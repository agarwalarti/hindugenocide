What is Cultural Genocide?

Cultural genocide does not always come with bloodshed or headlines. It is slower, and often masquerades as “modernization,” “reform,” or even “national interest.” But its consequences are no less severe.

The culture of a group of people is something that holds groups together, helps them practice their religion through generations, and preserves knowledge systems beyond just theoretical understanding of certain subjects. Culture includes, but is not limited to traditions. It includes mannerisms, dressing, rituals, gatherings, celebrations, language, holy books, and engagement of the community in numerous ways, either for study or for fun. Many indigenous groups have all of these elements in their culture, and strive to preserve them and protect them, because without that the next generations will not know their roots, nor the subtle values that are learnt via these cultural practices. Culture is a part of the identity of many people, something that gives them a sense of “grounding” in trying times, and in upheavals and catastrophes of all types.

What is Cultural Genocide?

Cultural genocide, in other words, is all the indirect and unfair means used to destroy the cultural heritage of an ethnic group, by legal or illegal means.

“Cultural genocide, signifying this latter type, is the systematic destruction of the specific cultural characteristics and heritage of a group. This could be done through the elimination of cultural symbols, artifacts, and institutions, as well as the prohibition of cultural activities or codes of behavior, forcible conversion, and demoralization. Lemkin’s framework of the genocidal process included this entire continuum, from the broader to narrower means of group destruction.”

— Jeff Benvenuto, What is Cultural Genocide? Understanding Group Destruction in Genocide Studies

Cultural genocide does not always come with bloodshed or headlines. It is slower, and often masquerades as “modernization,” “reform,” or even “national interest.” But its consequences are no less severe. When a community’s traditions, languages, religious practices, and modes of life are forcibly erased or systematically dismantled, the result is cultural annihilation—even if some members of the community remain physically alive.

Hindus, particularly in regions outside India, or even within India, have faced and continue to face acts that fall squarely under cultural genocide.

Destruction of Temples and Sacred Sites

One of the most visible aspects of Hindu cultural genocide is the repeated, deliberate destruction of temples—structures that are not just places of worship but repositories of art, philosophy, social gathering, and collective memory. In Pakistan and Bangladesh, thousands of Hindu temples have been desecrated, repurposed, or demolished entirely since Partition. The 1992 destruction of the Katas Raj temple pond ecosystem in Pakistan and the regular targeting of Durga Puja pandals in Bangladesh are not accidental. They are calculated acts of erasure.

Forcible Conversion and Suppression of Rituals

Forced conversions of Hindu girls and young women in Pakistan are not only human rights violations—they are also tools of cultural genocide. When a girl is taken away from her family, religion, and rituals, the cultural continuity of that family line is broken. In Afghanistan, where Hindus once thrived, decades of violence and pressure have forced nearly all of them to convert, flee, or die. Today, the Afghan Hindu community is virtually non-existent.

Erasure of Language and Knowledge Systems

Sanskrit, Tamil, and other ancient languages carry Hindu knowledge systems—philosophy, medicine, cosmology, literature, and law. In areas where Hindus are marginalized, these languages are not supported, and often actively excluded from the public education system. The same can be said of traditional Indian knowledge systems like Ayurveda or temple architecture, which are seen as “unscientific” or “backward” when removed from their context and stripped of their sacred legitimacy.

Demonization of Symbols and Practices

Bindi, tilak, sacred thread, vegetarianism, cow reverence, or even the recitation of Sanskrit shlokas—many of these practices are mocked, demonized, or banned in places where Hindus are a minority. In Malaysia, there have been instances of government interference in Hindu religious festivals like Thaipusam. In Pakistan, students are taught that cow worship is “idolatry,” and Hindu beliefs are inferior or false. In Bangladesh, even the wearing of traditional clothing by Hindu women has become unsafe in some areas. Within India, there are often bans or limits on the celebration of Hindu festivals, whereas festivals of other religions are rarely, if ever, proscribed. The Hindu sacred symbol of “swastika” is often conflated with the Hakenkreuz in many countries, leading to legal limits on Hindu rites and practices.

Displacement and Fragmentation of Communities

When communities are displaced—such as the Kashmiri Pandits from their homeland—they lose not just their homes, but their cultural micro-ecosystem. Sacred sites, shrines of family deities (kuldevtas), festivals connected to seasons or geography—all of it disappears or remains inaccessible. Even within India, where Hindus are the majority, certain smaller sects and traditions like Santhals, Devipujaks, or tribal Shaivite groups are facing increasing pressure to homogenize, convert, or abandon their practices.

Legal and Bureaucratic Obstacles

Cultural genocide also works through paperwork and laws. Hindu organizations in some countries face difficulties in registering religious trusts or acquiring land for temples. Curriculum guidelines exclude Hindu history, distort facts, or present colonizer narratives that degrade Hindu thought. Over time, these omissions change collective memory, severing younger generations from their heritage.

Why Cultural Genocide Matters

Cultural genocide is often the prelude to physical genocide. When a people’s culture is degraded and erased, they become easier to “other,” to scapegoat, and ultimately to eliminate. A people who cannot perform their rituals, speak their language, or teach their history are like a tree whose roots have been cut—they may stand for a while, but they will not last long.

For Hindus, the threat of cultural genocide is real and ongoing—not only in neighboring countries but even in some parts of the global diaspora, where “tolerance” is weaponized to suppress Hindu voices while other religious communities are given platforms. Recognizing cultural genocide is the first step toward resisting it—by documenting traditions, reviving languages, supporting indigenous arts, and telling the stories that others would rather silence.
